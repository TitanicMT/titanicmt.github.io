## The problem

How do you help people who commute long distances to work or school feel less isolated,
and address the safety concerns of travelling alone?

This started close to home: most of the team took public transit to campus, and the
journey was boring, tedious and meaningless. We wanted to fix our own commute.

## The pivot

My initial research direction was carpooling. That research is what killed it — I found
a crowded field of dedicated carpooling platforms the team had not known existed, which
undermined our whole premise.

I led the discussion on changing direction and proposed focusing on public-transit
commuters instead, a far less explored category. The team agreed, and that pivot defined
the rest of the project.

## What I did

**Competitor analysis.** Identified one direct competitor, SmartCommute.ca, and two
indirect ones, Uber and Facebook Communities. I tested SmartCommute's usability myself
and found four concrete failures: no built-in messaging, too much planning required
up front, difficult navigation, and excessive setup before any value appeared. Each
became a thing our design had to avoid.

**User research.** Interviewed two participants matching our target demographic of
frequent transit commuters, and analysed five of the team's ten interviews. We compiled
scripts into keywords and sorted them into affinity diagrams, using frequency to find
the pain points that actually recurred rather than the ones that were merely vivid.

**Journey mapping.** Built an AS-IS map of the current commuting experience from the
interview findings, and later a TO-BE map showing what the experience becomes with our
design in it.

**Design.** Created UI sketches and a task flow for route setup, combined into a
storyboard. The team sorted all our design ideas through a prioritisation matrix,
weighing implementation effort against impact on the experience.

**Visual direction.** From a team mood board, I designed the style tile: orange and
light blue, two contrasting colours that complement rather than compete, on white with
black type. The team adopted my style tile for the final design, and I built the
high-fidelity mock-ups from it.

**Usability testing.** Ran one testing session with the low-fidelity prototype, taking
notes as the participant navigated and asking follow-ups like *where there anything you
found confusing* and *what do you think this prototype is for*.

## What I learned

Flexibility mattered more than commitment. Pivoting from a generic ride-sharing concept
to a niche one was the single decision that made the project work.

The interviews were harder than expected — early responses were shallow, and the fix was
better follow-up questions rather than more participants. That skill transferred to
everything since.
